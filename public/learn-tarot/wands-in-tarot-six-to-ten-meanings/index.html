Wands in tarot: Six to Ten meanings

The Six to Ten of Wands follow the fire suit from recognition to overload. Each card has an upright and a reversed meaning.

By readmyspread5 November 2026

The second half of the suit of Wands moves from recognition to weight. Six to Ten of Wands, the fire cards of tarot, follow what happens after the early effort: success, defence, momentum, exhaustion and the load of too much responsibility. The descriptions below use the Rider-Waite-Smith (RWS) deck, and other decks may picture the cards differently.

Six of Wands

Recognition, victory, confidence. A rider wearing a laurel wreath moves through a crowd, with a wand crowned by another wreath. It is traditionally a card of public success or being acknowledged. Reversed, it can point to wavering confidence, a win that feels hollow, or a need for approval that outweighs the achievement.

Seven of Wands

Defending your position, perseverance, standing firm. A figure on higher ground holds a wand against six others rising from below. The card often asks what you are protecting and whether it is worth the effort. Reversed, it can suggest feeling overwhelmed, giving ground too easily, or a defensiveness that has outlasted the threat.

Eight of Wands

Speed, movement, news arriving. Eight wands fly diagonally through open sky with no figure in sight. It is a card of things falling into motion quickly, and many readers connect it with messages, travel or fast progress. Reversed, it often means delays, scattered effort or momentum that has stalled.

Nine of Wands

Resilience, caution, one more push. A bandaged figure leans on a wand, with eight more standing behind. Weariness and watchfulness sit together here. It can prompt you to ask how much you have already come through and what you still need to guard. Reversed, it may suggest exhaustion, stubbornness or an unwillingness to accept help.

Ten of Wands

Burden, responsibility, overload. A figure bent forward carries a bundle of ten wands towards a distant village. The card is traditionally about taking on too much. It does not say the task is wrong, only that the load may be heavy. Reversed, it can mean putting a burden down, delegating, or the opposite: struggling on when you could ask for help.

Reading the run as a story

These five cards form a rough arc: you are recognised, you defend what you have won, things move fast, you grow weary, and finally the weight is felt. Notice that the Ten does not signal failure. In the suit of fire it often marks the point at which effort needs to be shared or redirected.

In a spread

If a Ten of Wands lands in a position like "what to let go", it can be a prompt to look at what you are carrying out of habit. A Six in the "what helps" spot might simply invite you to take stock of what you have already achieved.

Keeping your own notes

Decks picture these cards in different ways, so it helps to jot down what you notice in your own deck. Write a line about what each figure seems to be doing and how that sits with your question. Over time your notes become a personal glossary that is more useful than any fixed list.

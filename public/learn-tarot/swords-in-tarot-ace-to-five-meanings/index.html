Swords in tarot: Ace to Five meanings

The Ace to Five of Swords run from clear thinking into strain, rest and conflict. Each card has an upright and a reversed meaning.

By readmyspread10 November 2026

Swords are the suit of air in tarot, linked with thought, communication and decisions. Ace to Five of Swords form a sequence that goes from clarity into strain: a clear idea, a stalemate, heartache, rest and conflict. Many readers find this suit the most challenging, but it is also one of the most honest. The descriptions below use the Rider-Waite-Smith (RWS) deck, and other decks may differ.

Ace of Swords

Clarity, truth, a breakthrough in thinking. A hand from a cloud holds an upright sword topped with a crown and wreath. It is a card of insight and of cutting through confusion. Reversed, it can suggest muddled thinking, harsh words, or a clear idea you have not yet acted on.

Two of Swords

Stalemate, difficult choice, avoidance. A blindfolded figure sits with two swords crossed over the chest, in front of a calm sea. Many readers see someone holding the balance by refusing to look. It can ask what you might not want to see. Reversed, it may point to indecision ending, information coming to light, or being overwhelmed by choice.

Three of Swords

Heartache, sorrow, honest pain. Three swords pierce a heart against a rainy sky. It is one of the most direct images in the deck and speaks to grief or hurt, often from words or separation. It is not a prediction of harm. It is a prompt to acknowledge a feeling. Reversed, it can suggest healing, release, or pain being held in rather than faced.

Four of Swords

Rest, recovery, pause. A figure lies as if on a tomb in a church, with three swords on the wall above and one beneath, and a stained-glass window nearby. Despite the sombre setting, the card is about rest and restoring your mind. Reversed, it may mean restlessness, burnout, or returning to activity before you are ready.

Five of Swords

Conflict, hollow victory, defeat. A figure collects swords while two others walk away, shoulders low. It poses the question of what a win costs. In a spread it can prompt you to consider whether an argument is worth having. Reversed, it may point to reconciliation, regret over how a disagreement went, or conflict that lingers.

Reading the run as a story

In order, the five cards form an arc: an idea arrives, you hold back from looking at it, it hurts, you rest, and then you argue about it. That is only one way to see them. The suit is not saying life must be difficult. It is saying that thinking carries weight, and that being honest about what is on your mind often comes before change.

In a spread

A Four of Swords in "what helps" can be a simple prompt to rest and step back, while a Two in "what is in the way" might ask what you are choosing not to see. Reading Swords with a kind tone helps: they describe the mind at work, not a sentence on your life.

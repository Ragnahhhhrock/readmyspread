Tarot comes with its own vocabulary, and a few words show up in almost every guide. This glossary gives plain definitions of the terms you are most likely to meet when learning tarot and reading a tarot spread. Where usage varies between readers or decks, the entry says so.

Spreads and positions

Spread. A layout of cards in a set pattern. Each place in the pattern has a meaning, so a spread turns a pile of cards into an answer to a question. The one-card, three-card and Celtic Cross spreads are common examples.

Position. A place in a spread with a stated meaning, such as "past", "challenge" or "advice". The same card is read differently depending on its position.

Querent. The person the reading is for. Also called the sitter or the reader's client. When you read for yourself, you are the querent.

Significator. A card chosen to stand for the querent or the question. Not every reader uses one, and many skip it entirely.

The cards

Arcana. From the Latin for secrets, this is the term for the two groups that make up a standard 78-card deck. The major arcana are 22 numbered cards, from The Fool (0) to The World (XXI), often tied to big themes. The minor arcana are 56 cards in four suits.

Suit. One of the four groups of minor arcana: Wands, Cups, Swords and Pentacles. Pentacles are sometimes called Coins, and Wands may be called Batons or Rods.

Court card. A Page, Knight, Queen or King. Some decks use different names, such as Princess and Prince. Court cards can represent people, parts of yourself or approaches.

Rider-Waite-Smith (RWS). The widely used deck first published in 1909, designed by Pamela Colman Smith under the direction of Arthur Edward Waite. Many guides and decks follow its imagery, and it is often the reference for meanings.

Direction and reading

Upright. A card facing you the right way up. It is read in its usual sense.

Reversed. A card that falls upside down. Some readers give it a changed or blocked meaning and some ignore direction altogether.

Shuffle, cut and draw. Mixing the deck, splitting it, and taking cards for a spread. Methods differ between readers.

Pull. Casual for drawing a card, as in "I pulled the Three of Cups".

Reading. The act of interpreting the cards in a spread. In this site's usage it is a reflective exercise, not a prediction.

Common phrases

Elemental dignity. A method of reading neighbouring cards by how their suit elements get along. It is optional and more advanced.

Card of the day. A single card drawn each morning as a prompt, a simple use of a one-card spread.

Do not worry about memorising everything at once. Most readers pick up these words naturally, and the first four, spread, position, arcana and upright or reversed, are enough to follow almost any beginner guide.

Tarot has no demonstrated ability to predict events. Readings are for entertainment and reflection only, and they aren't medical, legal or financial advice.

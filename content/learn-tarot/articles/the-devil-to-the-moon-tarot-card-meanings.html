The four tarot cards from the Devil (XV) to the Moon (XVIII) are some of the most striking in the major arcana. They deal with attachment, upheaval, hope and uncertainty, and they have a reputation for being frightening. In practice they are often the most useful cards in a reading, because they point at the things people find hard to say. Meanings here follow the Rider-Waite-Smith (RWS) deck.

The Devil (XV)

Two small figures are chained loosely to a horned figure on a pedestal. Attachment, constraint, temptation, the shadow side. The Devil is not about evil. It is about the ties we feel we cannot break: habits, dependencies, fears, or beliefs that limit us. Look closely at the chains in the RWS image and you will see they hang loose, which many readers take as a hint that these constraints can be loosened.

Reversed: breaking free, recognising a pattern, or reclaiming choice. It can also suggest the process is not finished.

The Tower (XVI)

Lightning strikes a tall tower, and figures fall from it. Upheaval, sudden change, revelation, structures giving way. The card suggests that something built on a weak foundation may come down, often bringing a clearer view. It is rarely comfortable, though it can also mark a release. Thinking of it as a prompt about what is no longer holding up is more useful than treating it as an omen.

Reversed: avoiding or delaying a necessary change, the aftermath of upheaval, or a smaller shake-up than expected.

The Star (XVII)

A figure kneels at a pool, pouring water onto the land and into the pool, under a large star surrounded by smaller ones. Hope, renewal, calm, openness. After the Tower, the Star is a quiet exhale. It asks what gives you a sense of purpose, and what small acts of care might help you restore your energy.

Reversed: discouragement, doubt, or feeling disconnected from what inspires you.

The Moon (XVIII)

A moon shines over a path between two towers, with a dog, a wolf and a crayfish near a pool. Intuition, illusion, uncertainty, the unconscious. Things are not fully visible here, and the card cautions against taking half-seen shapes for fact. It can prompt you to ask what you are assuming, what is a fear rather than a fact, and what your instincts are telling you.

Reversed: confusion beginning to clear, a fear loosening, or a tendency to avoid facing what is unclear.

Reading these cards in a spread

These four cards often provoke a strong reaction. A few ways to work with that:

  • Start with the image. What do you see before you reach for a meaning?
  • Check the position. The Devil as "what is in the way" is a natural fit. The Devil as "advice" asks something different, such as examining a habit.
  • Look at the neighbours. The Star beside the Tower, for instance, can read as hope following disruption.
  • Remember it is reflective. None of these cards forecasts an event.

Decks vary in imagery and sometimes in emphasis, so if your deck shows a different scene, trust what you see alongside the traditional meaning.

Tarot has no demonstrated ability to predict events. Readings are for entertainment and reflection only, and they aren't medical, legal or financial advice.
